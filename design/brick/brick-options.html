Three ways to add a brick calculator

All three use the same sums: brick 250 × 120 × 65 mm, 10 mm joints, 5% spare. A one-brick wall then takes about 103 bricks per m², and a half-brick wall about 51. The mortar is what is left of the wall's volume after the bricks.

Today it's done on paperThe app knows the net wall area (50,31 m² in the test room) but the mason still works out the bricks by hand and types the result.
The rule we keepEstimate numbers never change by themselves. In every option the result goes into the estimate only when you tap "Add", and it is a copy: later changes to a room don't touch it.
This changes the approved scopeThe spec defers automatic material quantities. Choosing an option means adding one line to the spec and one footnote to the PDF.
The norms are yours to confirmBrick sizes, joint, spare and cement per m³ below are my starting values. You know the local practice; each will be adjustable.
A

A helper inside the estimate

A "Brick calculator" button on the Estimate tab opens one sheet. Pick the walls of a measured room, the wall thickness and the brick. It shows bricks and mortar, and "Add to estimate" creates the rows with quantities filled in and prices left for you.

C

A brickwork sheet for the whole job

Each project gets a "G‘isht terish" page: a list of the walls to build. A wall comes from a measured room or is typed as length × height, each with its own thickness and openings. The page totals bricks, mortar, cement and sand, and adds them to the estimate in one tap.

Good for: how a mason actually quotes: wall by wall, with different thicknesses, whether or not the wall belongs to a measured room.

Good for: cement and sand alongside the bricks, and one place to see the whole job's masonry.

Trade-off: the biggest change. A new page and a new kind of saved data (walls to build), which also has to appear in the PDF and the backup. Cement and sand norms need your figures before I'd show them.

My recommendation: A, plus B's tool

A covers quoting from rooms you've measured with one button and no new screens. B adds the on-site answer for any single wall and reuses the same sums, so it costs little extra. Together they cover both moments a mason needs the number. C is the right shape if most of your jobs are free-standing walls rather than rooms; it's worth building later on top of A, once the norms are settled.
