Three ways to design the project page

All three show the same brickmason job: Yusupovlar uyi, with two workspaces (1st floor: living room and bedroom; bathroom), all 9 measurements confirmed, an estimate of bricks, cement, sand and labour (17 710 000 so‘m), and quote №1 sent 2 days ago.

A large table on every visitCustomer, phone, location and address take the top 40% of the screen, every time you open the project.
The numbers are hiddenNet wall area and how much is measured are nowhere on the project page.
No quick contactCalling the customer or opening the map means digging into the table.
A switch for one rowThe List / 2D / 3D switch sits above a list that often has one or two workspaces.
A

Compact header + tabs

A short header with Call and Map buttons, the three key numbers, then four tabs: Workspaces, Estimate, Quotes, Details. Contact details move to their own tab.

C

Quantities first

A "Hajmlar" (Quantities) tab opens first, with a table per room: net wall area and floor area, grouped by workspace, with totals. A button copies the total wall area into an estimate row.

Good for: the brickmason's real question, "how many square metres of wall?", answered on the first screen and ready for ordering bricks.

Good for: copying a measured area into the estimate is a deliberate tap, never automatic, so typed quantities aren't overwritten.

Trade-off: a table at phone width; long room names wrap.

My recommendation: A, with C's Quantities tab

Use A's compact header (Call, Map, three numbers) and add C's quantities table as the first tab: Hajmlar · Ish joylari · Smeta · Takliflar (Quantities · Workspaces · Estimate · Quotes). Contact details move to the ⋯ menu instead of a fifth tab. B's job path is clear, but it adds a tap to everything; its "Quote accepted → In progress" button could sit on the Quotes tab.
